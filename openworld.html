Open World

Open-world games give players access to large environments that can be explored with significantly more freedom than traditional level-based games. Rather than following one fixed path, players can often decide where to travel, which activities to complete and how they want to interact with the game world.

Core Idea //

The defining idea behind open-world design is freedom of movement. Large sections of the world are available for players to explore without constantly being restricted to a single route.

Main objectives may still guide progression, but players can often leave those objectives temporarily and explore other locations or activities.

Common Mechanics //

Exploration, side activities, large maps, optional objectives and multiple forms of transportation are commonly associated with open-world games.

Many also use dynamic systems such as weather, day-and-night cycles, non-player characters and events that make the world appear active beyond the main story.

History //

Ideas associated with open-world design appeared long before modern three-dimensional games. Some early games allowed players to explore environments in different directions rather than moving through a completely fixed sequence.

As computer and console hardware became more powerful, developers were able to create larger environments with more characters, objects and activities.

The move into 3D greatly expanded the possibilities of open-world design. Cities, countryside and other large environments could be represented as spaces that players physically travelled through.

Modern open-world games can contain enormous environments, optional missions, dynamic systems and hundreds of locations, allowing players to create their own route through the game.

Evolution of Open Worlds //

Early open environments were limited by memory, processing power and storage. Worlds often contained relatively simple objects and interactions.

Improvements in hardware allowed developers to increase both the physical size of their worlds and the amount of detail contained within them.

Open-world design gradually moved beyond simply providing a large map. Developers began filling these spaces with side missions, characters, environmental storytelling and interconnected gameplay systems.

Representative Games //

These games demonstrate different stages in the development of open-world game design.

2001

Grand Theft Auto III

Helped popularise the modern 3D open-world city, allowing players to freely explore Liberty City between missions.

2011

The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim

Created a large fantasy world where exploration, quests and character development allow players to choose their own path through the experience.

2017

The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild

Emphasised player-driven exploration and systemic interaction, allowing players to approach objectives in many different ways.

Why Open World Matters //

Open-world design changed the relationship between players and virtual environments. Instead of the game always deciding where the player must go next, exploration itself can become an important part of the experience.

This freedom can create individual experiences. Two players may enter the same world but discover locations, complete activities and approach objectives in completely different orders.
